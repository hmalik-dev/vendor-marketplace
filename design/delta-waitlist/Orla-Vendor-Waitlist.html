Why it isn't role-conditional
The invitation notice is about vendors, so it appears only for vendors. Consent is about signing up, which both roles do — so the small print is unconditional, and it renders in the disabled state too, before either card is picked.
Muted, not clay body

35Vendor sign-up — the invitation noticeMVPFrame 12 unchanged plus two lines: the invitation notice, shown only once the vendor card is selected, and the legal small print, shown for every role. Plain means plain — no banner fill, no icon, no gold. Gold would say waiting on you, and there is nothing here for you to do about it: this is a condition of the product, not a state of your account. The small print sits below it, where consent language belongs, and is the same for both roles.

Why the notice isn't a banner
A filled banner is a state: steel resolves itself, gold waits on someone, red failed. None of those is true of “by invitation for now” — it's how the product works this month. Plain body copy above a hairline rule is the honest weight, and it keeps the only fill on the panel where it belongs: the button.
Customer role shows one line, not two
Selecting I'm planning an event removes the invitation notice and keeps the small print — a customer signs up and lands in the product, so a waitlist sentence there would be a lie. The rule is: notice is role-conditional, small print never is.
No queue language anywhere
Never a position, a count, a length, or a date. “We'll add you to the waitlist” is the whole promise the screen is allowed to make, and it's repeated verbatim on the details screen so the two steps don't contradict each other.

37You're on the waitlist — the terminal screenMVPThe only screen in the product with nothing to do on it, and it has to look like that on purpose rather than look unfinished. So: the panel column becomes the whole frame, centred, and the photograph goes — the sell is over, this person already signed up. One sage mark, the saved address quoted back, three short sentences, and a plain text link home. No button, because a button asks for something. No progress bar, no “what happens next” timeline, no position, no date.

Sage, and only once
Per 40-states.md sage means settled — nothing outstanding, which is exactly this screen's claim. It appears once, as a 46px mark, and never as a full-width banner: a banner would imply there's a page underneath it still to deal with.
The address is quoted, not paraphrased
Shown in full and in ink, because the one thing a person can still get wrong at this point is the email itself — and this is the last moment they'd catch a typo before an invite goes to an address they can't read. Semibold ink, not clay; it's a fact, not a link.
No states, by design
Nothing on this screen submits, so there is no error, no spinner and no failed save — the save already succeeded on 36, and this screen is what proves it. If it's reached by returning later and the account read fails, that's the shell's steel couldn't load state from 26, not a variant of this screen.

Why the confirmation carries a details block and the invites don't
It's the only receipt a waitlisted vendor gets — there's no dashboard to check — so the four facts we hold are listed and a reply is offered as the correction channel. The invites need no receipt; they need one button.
No dates, no counts, no urgency
No “within X days”, no expiring invite, no position, no “you're one of N”. If the invite link ever does expire, that belongs in the link's failure screen, not in a sentence here that would be a timing promise.
One clay button, no second action
Both invites carry exactly one, and the confirmation carries none — the reply line is the action, and a Visit Orla button under “nothing else you need to do” would contradict the sentence above it.
Plain-text fallback is the same sentences
Each send is short enough to be its own plain-text part verbatim, button becoming a bare URL. Nothing here depends on the card, the mark or the rule to make sense.
